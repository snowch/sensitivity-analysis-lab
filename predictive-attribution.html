Plan a fair test on real logs

Decide how to test a delivery-time predictor and which columns to shuffle to see what it relies on.

Test the predictor on realistic unseen examples

  • Predict future deliveries. Train on earlier weeks and reserve later deliveries for testing.
  • Predict for new drivers. Train on some drivers and test on different drivers. Keep every record from the same driver together.

Fit any rules for filling missing values on the training rows only.

Compare the model’s error with a baseline that always predicts the median, the middle delivery time in the training data. The mean absolute error (MAE) averages how far each prediction is from the measured time. If you need to warn customers before the van leaves, flag a delivery when its predicted arrival is after the promised time. On held-out deliveries, count late deliveries the predictor missed and on-time deliveries it flagged by mistake.

Shuffle the signal you want to investigate

Distance and delivery zone can carry overlapping information. Shuffle them separately to see how much the fitted model relies on each. Shuffle them as a pair to see how much the model relies on their shared information. Use the same row order for both columns so their values stay aligned.

Even a paired shuffle can assign a distance and zone that could never occur on the recorded route. The added prediction error may reflect these implausible inputs. Try shuffling within groups of deliveries on compatible routes, and describe the resulting score as reliance within those groups. Repeat the shuffle to see whether another row order changes the score.

Choose the meaning of importance

Permutation importance
How much does test error change when a signal is shuffled? The result depends on the fitted model, the data, the error measure and the shuffle rule.
Sobol indices
How much output variance comes from individual inputs and their interactions? The classical calculation assumes independent inputs.
Shapley effects
How can we allocate the output variance across inputs while preserving their joint distribution?
Predictive Shapley explanations
How much does each input contribute to one prediction’s difference from a reference, such as the average across all deliveries?

Explain a 32-minute prediction

In this illustrative calculation, the predictor averages 20 minutes across its reference deliveries. With this delivery’s distance fixed, averaging the predictions over reference zones gives 26 minutes. With this delivery’s zone fixed, averaging over reference distances gives 24 minutes. Using both values for this delivery gives 32 minutes.

  • Distance. Knowing distance adds 26 − 20 = 6 minutes when neither input is known, or 32 − 24 = 8 minutes when zone is already known. Its credit is (6 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7 minutes.
  • Zone. Knowing zone adds 24 − 20 = 4 minutes when neither input is known, or 32 − 26 = 6 minutes when distance is already known. Its credit is (4 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5 minutes.

Distance and zone together account for the 12 minutes between the predictor’s 32-minute estimate for this delivery and the 20-minute reference. Changing the reference group or the way you handle unknown values can change the credits.

To estimate the effect of changing the route, run a controlled comparison or use a defensible causal design.
