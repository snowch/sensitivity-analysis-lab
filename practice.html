Independent practice

Stay within a household electricity budget

A household wants to stay at or below $200 in at least 95% of months. It can test one change. Use the results below to recommend which change to try first.

The model multiplies electricity use, in kilowatt-hours (kWh), by the price per kWh and adds $12. It counts 3 kWh per dryer cycle and 18 kWh per cold day multiplied by a heat-loss factor. It also includes other household use. For example, a factor of 1.2 raises estimated heating use by 20%.

The page samples each model input independently. In Usual conditions, a month can have 0–22 cold days. In Colder season, it can have 12–26. The two scenarios share the other ranges. The predictor uses a separate set of 3,000 synthetic records from usual conditions, with noisy bills and two measurements of cold weather: Outdoor-sensor cold days and Thermostat-request days.

Gather evidence for a recommendation

  1. Set Method to Spearman rank screen. With Model scenario set to Usual conditions, select Run analysis. Repeat for Colder season and read Top input across scenarios. Keep Colder season selected, switch Method to First- and total-order Sobol, and run again. Which input has the largest gap between its two bars?
  2. Set Method to Permutation importance. With Shuffle set to One feature at a time, select Run analysis. Compare Forest test MAE with Median-only test MAE. Switch Shuffle to Cold-weather signals together and run again. Use Weather signal comparison to compare the group with the individual signals.
  3. Set Method to Compare interventions and Model scenario to Usual conditions. Run two choices under Intervention, such as Reduce heat loss by 30% and Halve dryer cycles. Repeat both in Colder season. In Options across runs, check which values in Option over $200 are 5% or less.

Check your recommendation

  1. Model. Name the leading input under each range and explain why the ranking moved. Give a plausible interaction in the bill formula. State where real ranges could come from and what dependence would invalidate the independent-input analysis.
  2. Logs. Compare the model’s test error with the simple baseline. Compare individual and grouped shuffles. Explain what these scores measure and what evidence you’d need to estimate the effect of an intervention.
  3. Decision. Give the percentage of months over $200 for at least two alternatives and state which meet the target under each scenario. Propose a fair test, specify what changes and what you’d hold steady, and account for upfront costs before a real purchase.
Compare your work with a worked recommendation

Cold days lead the usual Spearman ranking. With the 12–26 day range, cold days vary less and the electricity rate ranks first. Heating demand multiplies cold days by the heat-loss factor, so a change in heat loss matters more during colder months. On the synthetic logs, the forest’s test MAE is lower than the median-only prediction’s test MAE. The two cold-weather signals share information, so shuffling them together costs more predictive accuracy than shuffling either alone.

In the usual model, about 15% of baseline months exceed $200. Reducing heat loss by 30% takes this to about 3.5%, while halving dryer cycles leaves about 12% over budget. In the colder case, even the heat-loss option leaves about 14% over budget. I’d test and measure the achievable heat-loss reduction before committing to the usual-case plan, and budget separately for a colder season.

To test a small, reversible change, compare heating electricity per cold day before and after it under similar conditions. Hold thermostat settings and occupancy as steady as possible and record weather, comfort and major load changes. I’d revise the estimate if the reduction were substantially below 30% after accounting for weather.
