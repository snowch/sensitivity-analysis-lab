Independent practice

Stay within a household electricity budget

A household wants its monthly bill to exceed $200 in at most 5% of months. It has time to try one change. Decide what to test, and explain where your evidence comes from.

For the bill model, add a $12 fixed charge to electricity use multiplied by the price per kilowatt-hour (kWh). Electricity use includes other household use, 3 kWh per dryer cycle, and 18 kWh per cold day multiplied by a heat-loss factor. For example, a factor of 1.2 raises estimated heating use by 20%.

The page samples each model input independently. In Usual conditions, a month can have 0–22 cold days. In Colder season, it can have 12–26. The two scenarios share the other ranges. The predictor uses a separate set of 3,000 synthetic records from usual conditions, with noisy bills and two measurements of cold weather.

Gather evidence for a recommendation

  1. Under Method, choose Spearman rank screen and select Run analysis. Run once with Model scenario set to Usual conditions and once with Colder season. Compare the first input in each ranking. Use First- and total-order Sobol to check which input has the largest gap between its bars.
  2. Choose Permutation importance and run with One feature at a time. Compare the two test errors and note both weather signals’ error increases. Select Cold-weather signals together and run again. Compare the group’s increase with the individual increases.
  3. Choose Compare interventions. Select and run two alternatives under Usual conditions. Use Over $200 to check the 5% limit. Change Model scenario to Colder season and rerun both alternatives. Which change, if any, meets the target in each scenario?

Check your recommendation

  1. Model. Name the leading input under each range and explain why the ranking moved. Give a plausible interaction in the bill formula. State where real ranges could come from and what dependence would invalidate the independent-input analysis.
  2. Logs. Compare the model’s test error with the simple baseline. Compare individual and grouped shuffles. Explain what these scores measure and what evidence you’d need to estimate the effect of an intervention.
  3. Decision. Give the percentage of months over $200 for at least two alternatives and state which meet the target under each scenario. Propose a fair test, specify what changes and what you’d hold steady, and account for upfront costs before a real purchase.
Compare your work with a worked recommendation

Cold days lead the usual Spearman ranking. The narrower 12–26 day range cuts their variation and the electricity rate takes first place. Heating demand multiplies cold days by the heat-loss factor, so a change in heat loss matters more during colder months. In the logs, test MAE improves over a median-only prediction. The two cold-weather signals share information, so shuffling them together costs more predictive accuracy than shuffling either alone.

In the usual model, about 15% of baseline months exceed $200. Reducing heat loss by 30% takes this to about 3.5%, while halving dryer cycles leaves about 12% over budget. In the colder case, even the heat-loss option leaves about 14% over budget. I’d test and measure the achievable heat-loss reduction before committing to the usual-case plan, and budget separately for a colder season.

For a pilot, compare heating electricity per cold day over comparable periods before and after a small, reversible change. Hold thermostat settings and occupancy as steady as possible and record weather, comfort and major load changes. I’d revise the estimate if the reduction were substantially below 30% after accounting for weather.
