Lesson 2 of 4

Change the assumptions

Test whether the same input stays important under two plausible sets of conditions.

Keep the formula and vary the range

Network round-trip time (RTT) is the time to send a request and receive a response. It is often measured in milliseconds (ms). A millisecond is one thousandth of a second.

In this model, each extra millisecond of RTT adds one millisecond to the job’s latency, the time it takes to finish.

An 8–12 ms range spans 4 ms. A 2–65 ms range spans 63 ms. The formula stays the same across both scenarios.

Choose input ranges that describe the situation you care about. A nearby network and a global rollout could produce different spreads. Measuring RTT where you plan to deploy helps you choose a relevant range.

Can a wider range change which input ranks first?

Read the result

Each millisecond of RTT contributes the same delay in both models. The wider range gives RTT more room to vary, so its ranking can rise. To estimate a saving, compare the delays before and after a specific change to RTT.

Check your reasoning

Network RTT ranks first under the wider range. What evidence would tell you whether that ranking applies to your deployment?

Why the range width and input dependence matter

A uniform draw gives equal chances to equal-width intervals. Its variance, a measure that gives more weight to large deviations from the mean, is the squared range width divided by 12. Widening the RTT range from 4 to 63 ms multiplies the network term’s variance by about 248. Its share of total variance also depends on the other inputs.

In an autoscaling service, worker capacity may rise with demand. Drawing the two independently can create high-load, low-capacity conditions that rarely occur. Sample measured pairs or model operating regimes separately when that dependence matters.
