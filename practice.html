Independent practice

Stay within a household electricity budget

A household wants to stay at or below $200 in at least 95% of months. It can test one change. Run the analyses and use the results to recommend which change to try first.

How the bills are calculated

The model multiplies electricity use, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), by the price per kWh and adds $12.

  • Each dryer cycle uses 3 kWh.
  • Heating uses 18 kWh per cold day times a heat-loss factor. A factor of 1.2 raises heating use by 20%.
  • The model also includes other household electricity use.

Where the data come from

The page prepares 8,192 simulated months and samples each bill-model input independently. A month can have 0–22 cold days in Usual conditions or 12–26 in Colder season. Both scenarios use the same ranges for the other inputs.

The predictor uses a separate set of 3,000 synthetic records from usual conditions. The records contain noisy bills and two measures of cold weather, Outdoor-sensor cold days and Thermostat-request days.

After the first run

  1. Compare seasons. Keep the rank screen selected. Change Model scenario to Colder season and run again. Read Top input across scenarios.
  2. Check for interactions. Keep Colder season selected. Switch Method to First- and total-order Sobol and run again. Which input has the largest gap between First-order and Total-order?
  3. Check the predictor against the baseline. Set Method to Permutation importance and Shuffle to One feature at a time. Run the analysis. Compare Forest test MAE with Median-only test MAE.
  4. Test the shared weather signal. Set Shuffle to Cold-weather signals together and run again. In Weather signal comparison, which shuffle gives the largest Increase in test MAE ($)?
  5. Test two ways to lower the bill. Set Method to Compare interventions. Run Reduce heat loss by 30% and Halve dryer cycles in each Model scenario. In Options across runs, check which values in Option over $200 are 5% or less.

Check your recommendation

  1. Model. Name the leading input under each range and explain why the ranking moved. Give a plausible interaction in the bill formula. State where real ranges could come from and what dependence would invalidate the independent-input analysis.
  2. Logs. Compare the model’s test error with the simple baseline. Compare individual and grouped shuffles. Explain what these scores measure and what evidence you’d need to estimate the effect of an intervention.
  3. Decision. Give the percentage of months over $200 for at least two alternatives and state which meet the target under each scenario. Propose a fair test, specify what changes and what you’d hold steady, and account for upfront costs before a real purchase.
Reveal a worked household recommendation

Which inputs matter?

Cold days lead the usual Spearman ranking. With the 12–26 day range, cold days vary less and the electricity rate ranks first. Heating demand multiplies cold days by the heat-loss factor, so a change in heat loss matters more during colder months.

On the synthetic logs, the forest has lower test MAE than the median baseline. The two cold-weather signals share information. Shuffling them together costs more accuracy than shuffling either alone.

Which option stays within budget?

In the usual model, about 15% of baseline months exceed $200. Reducing heat loss by 30% takes this to about 3.5%. Halving dryer cycles leaves about 12% over budget.

In the colder case, even the heat-loss option leaves about 14% over budget. I’d measure how much heat loss can be reduced before committing, and budget separately for a colder season.

What would you measure?

To test a small, reversible change, compare heating electricity per cold day before and after it under similar conditions. Hold thermostat settings and occupancy as steady as possible and record weather, comfort and major load changes. I’d revise the estimate if the reduction were substantially below 30% after accounting for weather.
