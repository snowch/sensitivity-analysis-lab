Apply it to your own decision

Use this field guide to plan a decision that depends on several uncertain inputs. Start with a small example you can calculate by hand.

1. Write the decision before picking a method

In your own notes, write one sentence with an action, an outcome and the point at which you would act. For example, “I’ll change routes if the chance of arriving after 9:00 drops below 1 in 10.”

List what you can control, what you can measure and the people or conditions your result should cover.

2. Match your evidence to your question

You need the effect of a specific small change
Choose a reference and a meaningful step. Hold the other inputs fixed. Find how much the output changes, divide by the input step and report the slope in output units per input unit. Inspect a response curve before extrapolating.
You have a formula and want a shortlist
List inputs, units and plausible ranges. Draw values, record outcomes, and compare Spearman correlations. Plot the input against the output and look for curves or separate clusters. Repeat with plausible alternative ranges. If effects vary by region or there are many inputs, consider a Morris screen before spending evaluations on a full variance decomposition.
You have a formula and suspect interactions
Choose an output tied to your decision, such as whether a job misses its deadline. If independent input distributions are defensible, compare first- and total-order Sobol indices. Inspect a grid of joint settings to find when changing one input has a larger effect at certain values of another. If your goal is to avoid late jobs, check which combinations take delay above the limit. With dependent inputs, consider allocations that preserve the joint distribution, and distinguish shared predictive information from a controllable effect.
You have logs and want to understand a predictor
Match the error measure to what you’ll do with the prediction. For delivery-time estimates, compare mean absolute error in minutes. For warnings about late deliveries, count missed warnings and false alarms. Compare the predictor with a simple baseline on the same held-out data. Shuffle one input column at a time. Shuffle similar columns together to check their shared contribution to prediction. State which people, places or time periods the test data represent, and name the error measure.
You need to know whether a particular change helps
Compare matched scenarios in a model. Validate with a controlled test where possible. Choose the conditions, what to measure, your target and any cost or safety limits before you look at results.

3. Try a different setting

DecisionA first investigationWhat could change the answer
Plan a commuteSimulate walking, waiting, and riding. Compare the chance of a late arrival under two routes.Evening train intervals and missed connections. Try weekday and weekend scenarios separately.
Staff a community eventModel arrivals, registrations per volunteer, and the risk of a wait over 10 minutes.A rush interacts with low staffing. Even if the average wait for the day looks acceptable, compare the chance of a wait over 10 minutes during the busiest half hour with each staffing option.
Understand delivery delaysFit a predictor on past deliveries, test on later weeks, and shuffle the fields on the test set.Route and distance can carry overlapping information. First compare test errors from the fitted predictor and a baseline that always predicts the median delivery time from the training data. Then shuffle route and distance together in the test rows and compare the fitted predictor’s error before and after the shuffle.

4. Keep a record someone else can check

Record your decision rule, data period, input ranges and sources, method, and one result with units. Name the assumption that most threatens your conclusion. Describe the next measurement or test and what result would reverse your decision.

Run the optional Node.js example locally

Use Node.js 20 or later and a local copy of the source repository.

  1. Run node assets/energy-example.mjs from the repository root.
  2. In assets/energy-example.mjs, find cold_days in factors. Change its range from [0, 22] to [0, 4]. Run the same command again.
  3. Compare the leading inputs by absolute rho in the rank table and by total in the Sobol table. Which input leads both after the range change?

The example treats inputs as independent. If independence fits your own model, change the input names and ranges in factors and the formula in evaluate.
