Field guide

Apply it to your own decision

Pick a decision affected by several uncertain inputs. Start with a small example you can calculate by hand.

1. Write the decision before picking a method

Write one sentence with an action, an outcome, and the point at which you would act. For example, “I’ll change routes if the chance of arriving after 9:00 drops below 1 in 10.” List what you can control, what you can only measure, and the people or conditions the result should cover.

2. Match your evidence to your question

You need the effect of a specific small change
Choose a reference and a meaningful step. Hold other inputs fixed, calculate the change and report the slope with units. Inspect a response curve before extrapolating.
You have a formula and want a shortlist
List inputs, units and plausible ranges. Draw values, record outcomes, and compare Spearman correlations. Plot the input against the output and look for curves or separate clusters. Repeat with plausible alternative ranges. If effects vary by region or there are many inputs, consider a Morris screen before spending evaluations on a full variance decomposition.
You have a formula and suspect interactions
Simulate the output that matters to you. If independent input distributions are defensible, compare first- and total-order Sobol indices. Inspect a small grid of joint conditions and explain which regime makes the interaction matter. With dependent inputs, consider allocations that preserve the joint distribution, and distinguish shared predictive information from a controllable effect.
You have logs and want to understand a predictor
Define the target and a meaningful error score. Set aside examples for testing and check that your predictor beats a simple baseline on those examples. Shuffle one input column at a time. Shuffle similar columns together to check their shared contribution to prediction. State which people, places or time periods the test data represent, and name the error measure.
You need to know whether a particular change helps
Compare matched scenarios in a model. Validate with a controlled test where possible. Choose the conditions, what to measure, your target and any cost or safety limits before you look at results.

3. Try a different setting

DecisionA first investigationWhat could change the answer
Plan a commuteSimulate walking, waiting, and riding. Compare the chance of a late arrival under two routes.Evening train intervals and missed connections. Try weekday and weekend scenarios separately.
Staff a community eventModel arrivals, registrations per volunteer, and the risk of a wait over 10 minutes.A rush interacts with low staffing. Check the busy interval even if the daily average looks fine.
Understand delivery delaysFit a predictor on past deliveries, test on later weeks, and shuffle the fields on the test set.Route and distance can carry overlapping information. Try a joint shuffle and compare to a simple baseline.

4. Keep a record someone else can check

Record your decision rule, data period, input ranges and sources, method, and one result with units. Name the assumption that most threatens your conclusion. Describe the next measurement or test and what result would reverse your decision.

Use the analysis code on a small model

If you have Node.js, copy or clone the source repository and run node assets/energy-example.mjs in its root. In your local copy, replace the input names and ranges in factors and the formula in evaluate. Run again and find the rank and Sobol tables in the terminal. The example samples all inputs independently. Use it when that assumption fits your question.
