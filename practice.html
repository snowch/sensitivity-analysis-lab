Independent practice

Stay within a household electricity budget

A household wants to stay at or below $200 in at least 95% of months. It can test one change. Run the analyses and use the results to recommend which change to try first.

How the bills are calculated

The model multiplies electricity use, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), by the price per kWh and adds $12.

  • Each dryer cycle uses 3 kWh.
  • Multiply cold days by 18 kWh per day and by the heat-loss factor. With 10 cold days and a factor of 1.2, heating uses 216 kWh.
  • The model also includes other household electricity use.

Where the data come from

The teaching model samples the bill inputs independently. A month can have 0–22 cold days in Usual conditions or 12–26 in Colder season. Both scenarios use the same ranges for the other inputs.

The predictor uses a separate set of 3,000 synthetic records from usual conditions. The records contain noisy bills and two measures of cold weather, Outdoor-sensor cold days and Thermostat-request days.

After the first run

  1. Compare seasons. Keep the rank screen selected. Set Model scenario to Colder season and run again. In Top input across scenarios, does First input change when the cold-day range changes?
  2. Check for interactions. Keep Colder season selected. Set Method to First- and total-order Sobol and run again. The page uses 8,192 sample pairs. Which input has the largest gap between First-order and Total-order?
  3. Check the predictor against the baseline. Set Method to Permutation importance and Shuffle to One feature at a time. Run again. Is Forest test MAE lower than Median-only test MAE on the test records?
  4. Test the shared weather signal. Set Shuffle to Cold-weather signals together and run again. In Weather signal comparison, which shuffle gives the largest Increase in test MAE ($)?
  5. Test two ways to lower the bill. Set Method to Compare interventions and Model scenario to Usual conditions. Set Intervention to Reduce heat loss by 30% and run. Then choose Halve dryer cycles and run again. Repeat both choices in Colder season. The page compares each option with its baseline on the same 8,192 simulated months. In Options across runs, which values in Option over $200 are 5% or less?

Check your recommendation

  1. Model. Name the leading input under each range and explain why the ranking moved. Give a plausible interaction in the bill formula. State where real ranges could come from and what dependence would invalidate the independent-input analysis.
  2. Logs. Compare the model’s test error with the simple baseline. Compare individual and grouped shuffles. Explain what these scores measure and what evidence you’d need to estimate the effect of an intervention.
  3. Decision. Give the percentage of months over $200 for at least two alternatives and state which meet the target under each scenario. Propose a fair test. Specify what changes and what you’d hold steady. Before a real purchase, compare the upfront cost with the bill savings over the number of months you expect the change to last.
Reveal a worked household recommendation

Which inputs matter?

Cold days lead the usual Spearman ranking. With the 12–26 day range, cold days vary less and the electricity rate ranks first. In this model, the same reduction in the heat-loss factor saves more heating electricity in a month with more cold days. Check Option over $200 to see whether more than 5% of colder-season bills still exceed $200.

On the synthetic logs, the forest has lower test MAE than the median baseline. The two cold-weather signals share information. Shuffling them together costs more accuracy than shuffling either alone.

Which option stays within budget?

In the usual model, about 15% of baseline months exceed $200. Reducing heat loss by 30% takes this to about 3.5%. Halving dryer cycles leaves about 12% over budget.

In the colder case, even the heat-loss option leaves about 14% over budget. Before paying for a change intended to reduce heat loss, I’d measure whether it can achieve the assumed 30% reduction. I’d use the colder-season bill estimates to set a separate budget for those months.

What would you measure?

To test a reversible way of reducing heat loss, such as temporary draught sealing, compare heating electricity per cold day before and after the change under similar conditions. Hold thermostat settings and occupancy as steady as possible and record weather, comfort and major load changes. I’d revise the estimate if the reduction were substantially below 30% after accounting for weather.
