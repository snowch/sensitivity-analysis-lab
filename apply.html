Field guide

Apply it to your own decision

Choose one real question where several uncertain factors compete for your attention. Start small enough that you can check the result by hand.

1. Write the decision before picking a method

Write one sentence with an action, an outcome, and the point at which you would act. For example, “I’ll change routes if the chance of arriving after 9:00 drops below 1 in 10.” List what you can control, what you can only measure, and the people or conditions the result should cover.

2. Match your evidence to your question

You have a formula and want a shortlist
List inputs, units and plausible ranges. Draw values, record outcomes, and compare Spearman correlations. Check the scatter for curves or distinct regimes. Repeat with plausible alternative ranges.
You have a formula and suspect interactions
Simulate the output that matters to you. If independent input distributions are defensible, compare first- and total-order Sobol indices. Inspect a small grid of joint conditions and explain which regime makes the interaction matter.
You have logs and want to understand a predictor
Define the target and a meaningful error score. Reserve held-out data and beat a simple baseline. Shuffle each predictor, then group substitutes when their joint contribution matters. State what population and score the result covers.
You need to know whether a particular change helps
Compare matched scenarios in a model. Validate with a controlled test where possible. Choose the workload, metric, target and guardrails before looking at results.

3. Try a different setting

DecisionA first investigationWhat could change the answer
Plan a commuteSimulate walking, waiting, and riding. Compare the chance of a late arrival under two routes.Evening train intervals and missed connections. Try weekday and weekend scenarios separately.
Staff a community eventModel arrivals, registrations per volunteer, and the risk of a wait over 10 minutes.A rush interacts with low staffing. Check the busy interval even if the daily average looks fine.
Understand delivery delaysFit a predictor on past deliveries, test on later weeks, and shuffle the fields on the test set.Route and distance can carry overlapping information. Try a joint shuffle and compare to a simple baseline.

4. Keep a record someone else can check

Record your decision rule, data period, input ranges and sources, method, and one result with units. Name the assumption that most threatens your conclusion. Describe the next measurement or test and what result would reverse your decision.

Use the analysis code on a small model

If you use JavaScript, run node assets/energy-example.mjs from the source repository. The example shows how to pass labelled inputs, a sampler and an outcome function to the same rank and Sobol routines used in the course. Replace the formula and ranges with your own, and keep the independent-input assumption in view.
